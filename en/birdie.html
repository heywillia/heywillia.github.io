Birdie Apparel · Brand and product design · 2021 — 2022

Six capsules, one brand.

In apparel the brand isn't applied on the product: it is the product. Each drop needed graphics with a life of their own, and all of them had to remain Birdie.

01
The approach

A fixed logo would have killed the brand; six loose logos would have too.

The way out was to separate two layers. Underneath, a parent brand that is never touched: the handwritten logotype, the B in a circle and a single color rule —white on black, always. On top, free graphics per capsule, with permission to change typographic register completely.

What holds the whole together isn't a style, it's the application system: small chest print on the front, B on the back, label on the sleeve. The same garment, the same photo, the same distance. The drawing changes and nothing else.

02 · The parent brand

The B in a circle is what holds up at small size: embroidered on the back, woven into the sleeve label, avatar in the feed. It's the signature that lets the front do anything.

The stroke is deliberately irregular —a circle closed by hand, not a perfect ring. On a skate brand, geometric neatness would read as corporate.

@birdie.cloth ↗
03 · The six capsules

Each drop brings its own language: retro display, line-art, drawn lettering, distortion, fine script. Side by side they look like six brands; on the hanger they're the same one.

04 · Worn

Proof that the system works isn't in the presentation: it's on the street, with the garment worn and the logo twelve centimeters from the chest.

What it left me

A brand can change style every season if the application system doesn't move.

It's the opposite of the classic manual, which fixes the style and frees the rest. Here the constant is where each thing goes, not how it's drawn. That criterion later served me in packaging: at Thiafel the can does the same job as the hanger.
